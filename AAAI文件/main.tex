\begin{abstract}
Bundle recommendation (BR) aims to match users with collections of items that suit their preferences. Existing graph-based methods learn bundle preferences primarily from user-bundle (U-B) interactions, while incorporating user-item (U-I) interactions and bundle-item (B-I) affiliations as auxiliary information. Despite their effectiveness, limitations in the observed U-B interactions still pose two challenges: (1) noise in the observed U-B graph can introduce misleading preference signals during graph propagation; (2) sparse U-B feedback hinders accurate modeling of users' bundle preferences. To address these challenges, we propose ReCoBR. ReCoBR reduces the impact of noisy interactions through Pretraining-Based Diffusion-Driven U-B Graph Reconstruction (PDGR) and improves preference learning under sparse feedback through Cross-View Contrastive Learning (CCL). In PDGR, we first pretrain user and bundle embeddings on the observed U-B graph. We construct users' historical preference representations from the bundle embeddings and denoise the preference representations with a user-conditioned diffusion model. We then use the denoised representations to filter observed U-B interactions and reconstruct the U-B graph. Next, CCL applies contrastive learning between the reconstructed U-B view and each auxiliary view before representation fusion, enabling auxiliary information to better support bundle preference learning for sparse users. Experiments on three public datasets show that ReCoBR outperforms the baseline methods.
\end{abstract}


\section{Introduction}

Bundle recommendation (BR) aims to match users with collections of related items, such as music playlists and fashion outfits~\cite{chen2019dam,ma2022crosscbr}. BR helps users discover suitable collections while creating additional sales opportunities for platforms~\cite{ma2022crosscbr,ma2024multicbr}. BR methods commonly use three types of relations~\cite{ma2022crosscbr,ma2024multicbr}: user-bundle (U-B) interactions reflect users' choices of bundles; user-item (U-I) interactions provide information about their interests in individual items; and bundle-item (B-I) affiliations describe which items belong to each bundle. Together, these relations provide information for learning users' bundle preferences.

Early BR methods explored how to incorporate item-level information into bundle preference learning. For example, DAM jointly models U-B and U-I interactions, using attention to construct bundle representations from item embeddings~\cite{chen2019dam}. To capture higher-order relations among users, bundles, and items, BGCN introduces graph propagation in the bundle and item views. BGCN also uses B-I affiliations to aggregate item information into bundle representations~\cite{chang2020bgcn}. Building on multi-view representation learning, subsequent studies have focused on strengthening information exchange and integration across views. For instance, CrossCBR applies contrastive learning to align representations of the same user (bundle) across the bundle and item views~\cite{ma2022crosscbr}. Meanwhile, DGMAE uses knowledge distillation to transfer information from first- and higher-order U-B relations to the item view~\cite{ren2023dgmae}. More recently, MultiCBR separately learns representations from the U-B, U-I, and B-I views, fuses these representations for recommendation, and applies contrastive learning after fusion~\cite{ma2024multicbr}. Recent studies have also investigated diffusion models to improve user and bundle representations for recommendation. Specifically, DCDL applies diffusion learning to user representations within a dual-view framework to improve recommendation diversity~\cite{chen2025dcdl}. In addition, RDiffBR uses residual diffusion to adapt bundle representations to changes in bundle composition~\cite{zhang2026rdiffbr}.

Although these methods differ in their modeling strategies, they learn users' bundle preferences primarily from observed U-B interactions. However, noise and sparsity in the observed U-B interactions remain significant obstacles to accurately modeling users' bundle preferences. For example, accidental clicks may be recorded as positive interactions even when they do not reflect users' actual preferences. During graph propagation, misleading preference signals from noisy U-B interactions can spread through neighborhood aggregation. Meanwhile, many users have interacted with only a few bundles, leaving limited historical information for learning their preferences. For these users, item interests and bundle composition can provide additional information, but using this information effectively remains difficult. Based on the above analysis, we identify the following two challenges.

\textbf{Challenge 1: Denoising U-B Feedback While Preserving Preference Information.} The observed U-B graph does not indicate which connections are noisy, making it difficult to decide which ones to retain for graph propagation. Retaining unreliable connections may allow misleading preference signals to spread through neighborhood aggregation. However, removing too many connections can also discard valuable information about users' historical bundle choices. The challenge is to develop an effective mechanism for assessing the reliability of observed U-B interactions to guide graph denoising.

\textbf{Challenge 2: Cross-View Collaboration under Sparse U-B Feedback.} Sparse U-B interactions hinder accurate modeling of users' bundle preferences. To alleviate U-B sparsity, it is important to effectively use the additional information provided by U-I interactions and B-I affiliations. Existing three-view methods such as MultiCBR separately learn representations through graph propagation in each view and integrate these representations through weighted fusion~\cite{ma2024multicbr}. However, simple weighted fusion lacks direct cross-view constraints that can provide additional supervision for preference learning under sparse U-B feedback. The challenge is to make more effective use of information from the U-I and B-I views to improve bundle preference learning under sparse U-B feedback.

To address these challenges, we propose \textbf{ReCoBR (Reconstruction and Contrastive Learning for Bundle Recommendation)}, which combines diffusion-driven U-B graph reconstruction with cross-view contrastive learning. We first pretrain user and bundle embeddings on the observed U-B graph using LightGCN. For each user, we average the embeddings of bundles they have interacted with to construct a preference representation. We add Gaussian noise to each preference representation and train a diffusion model to recover it. The model is conditioned on the user's pretrained embedding. During denoising, we introduce a bundle-set consistency loss to encourage agreement between the recovered preference and the user's historical bundle embeddings. The trained denoiser iteratively refines each user's preference representation. Using these refined preferences, we score each user's observed U-B connections and retain the highest-scoring ones to reconstruct the U-B graph for propagation. Next, we encode the U-I, B-I, and reconstructed U-B views through graph propagation to obtain user and bundle representations. Before fusion, we use cross-view contrastive learning to align user and bundle representations from the reconstructed U-B view with those from the U-I and B-I views. The aligned representations are then combined to predict users' preferences for bundles.

Our main contributions are summarized as follows:

\begin{enumerate}
\item We propose ReCoBR, a bundle recommendation framework that uses diffusion-driven U-B graph reconstruction to reduce the impact of noisy interactions.
\item We design cross-view contrastive learning based on the reconstructed U-B view to improve bundle preference learning under sparse U-B feedback.
\item Experiments on three public datasets show that ReCoBR outperforms baseline methods and demonstrate the effectiveness of its main components.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}

\subsection{Bundle Recommendation}

Bundle recommendation includes both ranking predefined bundles and constructing new item collections~\cite{sun2024survey}. For bundle ranking, early methods combine U-B feedback with item-level information through factorization and attention-based learning~\cite{cao2017efm,chen2019dam}. DAM uses attention to aggregate item representations within a bundle, while BGCN learns from the bundle and item views through graph propagation~\cite{chang2020bgcn}.

Later studies examine different aspects of bundle preference learning. MIDGN models user intents in global and local views~\cite{zhao2022midgn}, while BundleGT uses hierarchical graph Transformers to capture bundling strategies~\cite{wei2023bundlegt}. Other methods focus on making better use of item-level information. DGMAE transfers information from direct and higher-order U-B relations to the item view through knowledge distillation~\cite{ren2023dgmae}. EBRec enhances item-level bundle representations by modeling bundle--user--item correlations and augmenting U-I interactions~\cite{du2025ebrec}. EpicCBR explores item relations to improve recommendation for newly introduced bundles~\cite{li2026epiccbr}.

\subsection{Denoising and Diffusion for Recommendation}

Implicit feedback can include interactions that do not reflect users' actual preferences. Early denoising methods reduce the influence of such interactions by removing selected training examples or assigning them lower weights~\cite{wang2021adt}. DeCA uses agreement between models to guide denoising~\cite{wang2022deca}. CAFU reduces noise through filtered uniformity and improves learning from sparse interactions through constrained alignment~\cite{jiang2026cafu}.

Diffusion models learn to recover data or representations from controlled perturbations~\cite{ho2020ddpm}. DiffRec models users' interaction vectors through diffusion~\cite{wang2023diffrec}, while DiffuRec uses diffusion to recover target-item embeddings in sequential recommendation~\cite{li2023diffurec}. DDRM applies multi-step denoising to make learned representations more robust to noisy feedback~\cite{zhao2024ddrm}.

Diffusion-based methods have also been applied to bundle recommendation. DCBR uses conditional diffusion to denoise U-B interactions~\cite{li2025dcbr}. DCDL combines dual-view modeling with diffusion on user representations, using fused user features to guide denoising and improve recommendation diversity~\cite{chen2025dcdl}. RDiffBR uses residual diffusion to adapt bundle representations when their item composition changes~\cite{zhang2026rdiffbr}. ReCoBR uses diffusion to refine preference representations built from users' historical bundle interactions. It then uses these representations to select observed U-B connections for graph propagation.

\subsection{Contrastive Learning for Recommendation}

Contrastive learning adds supervision by encouraging related representations to be similar~\cite{chen2020simclr}. In graph-based recommendation, SGL constructs different views through random changes to the interaction graph~\cite{wu2021sgl}. AdaGCL learns augmented views using graph generation and denoising models~\cite{jiang2023adagcl}, while BIGCF applies contrastive regularization to interaction and intent representations~\cite{zhang2024bigcf}.

In bundle recommendation, CrossCBR aligns representations of the same users and bundles across the bundle and item views before fusion~\cite{ma2022crosscbr}. MultiCBR models the U-B, U-I, and B-I views and applies contrastive learning after combining their representations~\cite{ma2024multicbr}. ReCoBR uses the reconstructed U-B view to align user and bundle representations with those from the U-I and B-I views before fusion.






\section{Methodology}\label{sec:methodology}

\subsection{Task Definition and Framework Overview}

Let $\mathcal U$, $\mathcal B$, and $\mathcal I$ denote the sets of users, predefined bundles, and items, respectively. The available training data comprise three binary matrices: user-bundle interactions $\mathbf X\in\{0,1\}^{|\mathcal U|\times|\mathcal B|}$, user-item interactions $\mathbf Y\in\{0,1\}^{|\mathcal U|\times|\mathcal I|}$, and bundle-item affiliations $\mathbf Z\in\{0,1\}^{|\mathcal B|\times|\mathcal I|}$. In $\mathbf X$ and $\mathbf Y$, an entry equal to one denotes an observed interaction; in $\mathbf Z$, it denotes bundle membership. Given these relations, our task is to estimate a preference score $\hat y_{ub}$ for each unobserved user-bundle pair and rank candidate bundles for each user.

Figure~\ref{fig:framework} outlines ReCoBR. To address noise in U-B interactions, we first pretrain user and bundle representations, learn a user-conditioned denoiser over historical bundle representations, and use the refined preferences to reconstruct the U-B graph. The denoising objective incorporates a bundle-set consistency loss (SetLoss) to retain bundle-preference information during recovery (Figure~\ref{fig:denoising}). To address sparse U-B feedback, we use cross-view contrastive learning to align representations from the reconstructed U-B view with those from the U-I and B-I views before fusion. The fused user and bundle representations produce the final recommendation scores.

We distinguish the observed matrix $\mathbf X$ from the reconstructed matrix $\widetilde{\mathbf X}$ throughout this section. The former defines users' training histories and ranking examples; the latter determines the U-B connections used for recommendation graph propagation.

\subsection{Diffusion-Driven U-B Graph Denoising and Reconstruction}\label{sec:graph-reconstruction}

This module evaluates observed U-B interactions based on users' historical bundle preferences. We construct a preference representation from each user's history, refine it through conditional denoising, and use it to select connections for graph propagation.

\subsubsection{Pretraining and Historical Preference Construction}

We pretrain LightGCN \cite{he2020lightgcn} on the observed U-B graph using Bayesian Personalized Ranking (BPR) \cite{rendle2009bpr}. Let $\mathbf h_u^{(0)},\mathbf h_b^{(0)}\in\mathbb R^d$ be its trainable initial embeddings, and let $\mathcal N_u=\{b\in\mathcal B:x_{ub}=1\}$ and $\mathcal N_b=\{u\in\mathcal U:x_{ub}=1\}$ denote the observed neighbors of user $u$ and bundle $b$. LightGCN propagation is given by
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\mathbf h_u^{(\ell+1)}
&=\sum_{b\in\mathcal N_u}
\frac{\mathbf h_b^{(\ell)}}
{\sqrt{|\mathcal N_u||\mathcal N_b|}},\\
\mathbf h_b^{(\ell+1)}
&=\sum_{u\in\mathcal N_b}
\frac{\mathbf h_u^{(\ell)}}
{\sqrt{|\mathcal N_b||\mathcal N_u|}}.
\end{aligned}
\tag{1}
\end{equation}
Here, $L_p$ is the number of pretraining propagation layers, and the fixed aggregation coefficients satisfy $\eta_\ell\geq0$ and $\sum_{\ell=0}^{L_p}\eta_\ell=1$. We aggregate the initial and propagated embeddings as
\begin{equation}
\mathbf e_u^0=\sum_{\ell=0}^{L_p}\eta_\ell\mathbf h_u^{(\ell)},
\qquad
\mathbf e_b^0=\sum_{\ell=0}^{L_p}\eta_\ell\mathbf h_b^{(\ell)}.
\tag{2}
\end{equation}
The resulting embeddings $\mathbf e_u^0$ and $\mathbf e_b^0$ remain fixed during graph reconstruction. They are subsequently used to construct historical preferences, condition the denoiser, and score observed interactions.

For each active user $u$, we summarize the representations of the historically interacted bundles by mean pooling:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf p_{u,0}
=\frac{1}{|\mathcal N_u|}
\sum_{b\in\mathcal N_u}\mathbf e_b^0.
\tag{3}
\end{equation}
The vector $\mathbf p_{u,0}$ represents the user's observed bundle-preference context. Mean pooling accommodates histories of different lengths while keeping the denoising input $d$-dimensional. We construct this vector for users in $\mathcal U_+=\{u\in\mathcal U:|\mathcal N_u|>0\}$. The subscript $0$ denotes the state before artificial noise is added; it does not assume that the observed history is free of interaction noise.

\subsubsection{User-Conditioned Diffusion Denoising}

We train the denoiser to recover historical preference representations under different levels of perturbation. Following the Gaussian diffusion formulation \cite{ho2020ddpm,wang2023diffrec}, let $T$ be the number of diffusion steps and $\beta_t\in(0,1)$ the noise variance at step $t$. We use a linear variance schedule and define $\alpha_t=1-\beta_t$ and $\bar\alpha_t=\prod_{s=1}^{t}\alpha_s$. A perturbed preference vector can be sampled directly as
\begin{equation}
\mathbf p_{u,t}
=\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,\mathbf p_{u,0}
+\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\boldsymbol\epsilon,
\qquad
\boldsymbol\epsilon\sim\mathcal N(\mathbf 0,\mathbf I_d).
\tag{4}
\end{equation}
Here, $\boldsymbol\epsilon\in\mathbb R^d$ is standard Gaussian noise, $\mathbf I_d$ is the $d$-dimensional identity matrix, and $\bar\alpha_t$ controls how much historical information remains at noise level $t$.

To make recovery personalized, we condition the denoiser on the pretrained user representation $\mathbf e_u^0$. We implement the denoiser $f_\theta$ as a multilayer perceptron (MLP), with parameters $\theta$, that predicts the unperturbed preference vector:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\widehat{\mathbf p}_{u,0}
&=f_\theta(\mathbf p_{u,t},\mathbf e_u^0,t),\\
f_\theta(\mathbf p,\mathbf c,t)
&=\operatorname{MLP}_\theta
\bigl([\mathbf p;\mathbf c;\mathbf p\odot\mathbf c;
|\mathbf p-\mathbf c|;\boldsymbol\psi(t)]\bigr).
\end{aligned}
\tag{5}
\end{equation}
In Eq. (5), $[\cdot;\cdot]$ denotes concatenation, $\odot$ denotes element-wise multiplication, and $|\mathbf p-\mathbf c|$ is the element-wise absolute deviation. The vectors $\mathbf p$ and $\mathbf c$ preserve the current preference estimate and the user condition, while $\mathbf p\odot\mathbf c$ and $|\mathbf p-\mathbf c|$ expose their coordinate-wise agreement and discrepancy. The sinusoidal embedding $\boldsymbol\psi(t)\in\mathbb R^{d_t}$ specifies the perturbation level. Together with the timestep embedding, these features allow the MLP to adapt preference recovery to both the user and the perturbation level. Consequently, the MLP receives a $(4d+d_t)$-dimensional input and predicts a $d$-dimensional preference vector.

During training, we uniformly sample $u\in\mathcal U_+$ and $t\in\{1,\ldots,T\}$ and draw Gaussian noise as in Eq. (4). The reconstruction objective is
\begin{equation}
\mathcal L_{\mathrm{rec}}
=\mathbb E_{u,t,\boldsymbol\epsilon}
\left[
\frac{1}{d}
\left\|\widehat{\mathbf p}_{u,0}-\mathbf p_{u,0}\right\|_2^2
\right].
\tag{6}
\end{equation}
This objective learns a shared recovery function from perturbed historical preferences. The original representation supplies the reconstruction target, while the pretrained user embedding provides personalized context.

\subsubsection{Bundle-Set Consistency in Denoising}

Reconstructing the pooled vector provides a basic denoising objective, but subsequent interaction selection depends on the recovered vector's compatibility with individual historical bundles. We therefore introduce SetLoss to connect preference recovery with the bundle representations that will be used for graph reconstruction. Figure~\ref{fig:denoising} illustrates how SetLoss complements the reconstruction objective in denoiser training.

Let $\operatorname{sim}(\mathbf a,\mathbf b)=\mathbf a^\top\mathbf b/(\|\mathbf a\|_2\|\mathbf b\|_2)$ denote cosine similarity, with a small numerical safeguard for zero norms. SetLoss encourages high average cosine similarity between the recovered preference and the user's historical bundle embeddings:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal L_{\mathrm{set}}
=\mathbb E_{u,t,\boldsymbol\epsilon}
\left[
\frac{1}{|\mathcal N_u|}
\sum_{b\in\mathcal N_u}
\left(1-\operatorname{sim}
\left(\widehat{\mathbf p}_{u,0},\mathbf e_b^0\right)\right)
\right].
\tag{7}
\end{equation}
Equation (6) reconstructs the pooled preference vector, whereas Eq. (7) aligns the recovered preference with its constituent historical bundles under the cosine-similarity geometry later used for interaction scoring. Averaging over the history prevents users with more interactions from dominating the objective.

We optimize the denoiser with
\begin{equation}
\mathcal L_{\mathrm D}
=\mathcal L_{\mathrm{rec}}
+\lambda_{\mathrm{set}}\mathcal L_{\mathrm{set}},
\tag{8}
\end{equation}
where $\lambda_{\mathrm{set}}\geq 0$ controls the contribution of SetLoss.

\subsubsection{Preference Refinement and Graph Reconstruction}

After learning the denoiser, we use it to refine the historical preference vector for interaction scoring. This stage employs a deterministic refinement procedure initialized with $\mathbf p_{u,0}$. Specifically,
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\mathbf r_{u,T}&=\mathbf p_{u,0},\\
\mathbf r_{u,t-1}
&=f_\theta(\mathbf r_{u,t},\mathbf e_u^0,t),
\qquad t=T,T-1,\ldots,1,\\
\mathbf p_u^\star&=\mathbf r_{u,0}.
\end{aligned}
\tag{9}
\end{equation}
The states $\mathbf r_{u,t}$ are refinement iterates, distinct from the forward-corrupted training states $\mathbf p_{u,t}$. Each iteration feeds the current estimate back into the denoiser with the next timestep condition. Thus, graph reconstruction applies the learned recovery network to an observed preference context, without sampling a Gaussian starting state.

We measure how closely each observed bundle matches the refined preference:
\begin{equation}
s_{ub}
=\operatorname{sim}(\mathbf p_u^\star,\mathbf e_b^0),
\qquad b\in\mathcal N_u.
\tag{10}
\end{equation}
For each user, we retain the highest-scoring $\kappa_u=\min(\kappa,|\mathcal N_u|)$ bundles, where the positive integer $\kappa$ controls the maximum retained degree:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\widetilde{\mathcal N}_u
&=\underset{b\in\mathcal N_u}{\operatorname{TopK}}
\left(s_{ub},\kappa_u\right),\\
\widetilde x_{ub}
&=\mathbf 1\!\left[b\in\widetilde{\mathcal N}_u\right].
\end{aligned}
\tag{11}
\end{equation}
Here, $\operatorname{TopK}(s_{ub},\kappa_u)$ returns the set of $\kappa_u$ bundles in $\mathcal N_u$ with the largest scores, and $\mathbf 1[\cdot]$ is the indicator function. Users with at most $\kappa$ observed bundles retain their full histories; users outside $\mathcal U_+$ have no retained U-B edges. Selection is restricted to observed interactions, so graph reconstruction neither creates new edges nor changes the ranking labels defined by $\mathbf X$. The resulting binary matrix $\widetilde{\mathbf X}$ is the reconstructed U-B graph used for downstream propagation.

\subsection{Pre-Fusion Cross-View Contrastive Learning}

With the U-B graph reconstructed, we next learn representations from this graph together with user-item behavior and bundle composition. When U-B feedback is sparse, U-I interactions and B-I affiliations provide additional information for preference learning. We learn user and bundle representations from each view and use cross-view contrastive learning to align representations from the reconstructed U-B view with those from the U-I and B-I views before fusion.

\subsubsection{View-Specific Representations}

Following the three-view backbone of MultiCBR \cite{ma2024multicbr}, we construct the U-B, U-I, and B-I propagation graphs from $\widetilde{\mathbf X}$, $\mathbf Y$, and $\mathbf Z$, respectively. Each encoder applies $L$ layers of symmetrically normalized LightGCN-style propagation \cite{he2020lightgcn}. Fixed view-specific coefficients combine the initial embeddings with the $\ell_2$-normalized layer outputs. During training, the inherited backbone adds small representation perturbations to the propagation outputs and auxiliary item pooling; these perturbations are disabled during evaluation. The encoders share trainable initial embeddings for the same entity, which are separate from the fixed pretrained embeddings in Section~\ref{sec:graph-reconstruction}.

\textbf{U-B view.} Propagation on the reconstructed U-B graph directly produces user representations $\mathbf e_u^{\mathrm{UB}}$ and bundle representations $\mathbf e_b^{\mathrm{UB}}$, capturing collaborative information through the retained interactions.

\textbf{U-I view.} Propagation on the U-I graph produces user representations $\mathbf e_u^{\mathrm{UI}}$ and item representations $\mathbf e_i^{\mathrm{UI}}$. To obtain a bundle representation $\mathbf e_b^{\mathrm{UI}}$ in this view, we average the representations of its constituent items according to $\mathbf Z$.

\textbf{B-I view.} Propagation on the B-I graph produces bundle representations $\mathbf e_b^{\mathrm{BI}}$ and item representations $\mathbf e_i^{\mathrm{BI}}$. To obtain a user representation $\mathbf e_u^{\mathrm{BI}}$ in this view, we average the representations of the items that the user has interacted with according to $\mathbf Y$.

The two item-pooling operations are
\begin{equation}
\mathbf e_b^{\mathrm{UI}}
=\frac{1}{|\mathcal I_b|}
\sum_{i\in\mathcal I_b}\mathbf e_i^{\mathrm{UI}},
\qquad
\mathbf e_u^{\mathrm{BI}}
=\frac{1}{|\mathcal I_u|}
\sum_{i\in\mathcal I_u}\mathbf e_i^{\mathrm{BI}},
\tag{12}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal I_b=\{i:z_{bi}=1\}$ contains the items in bundle $b$, and $\mathcal I_u=\{i:y_{ui}=1\}$ contains the items interacted with by user $u$. An empty neighborhood contributes a zero vector.

This step yields three representations for each user, $\{\mathbf e_u^{\mathrm{UB}},\mathbf e_u^{\mathrm{UI}},\mathbf e_u^{\mathrm{BI}}\}$, and each bundle, $\{\mathbf e_b^{\mathrm{UB}},\mathbf e_b^{\mathrm{UI}},\mathbf e_b^{\mathrm{BI}}\}$, all in $\mathbb R^d$. These unfused representations are used to construct the cross-view objective below and are later fused for prediction.

\subsubsection{Cross-View Contrastive Objectives}

For each user, we pair its representation from the reconstructed U-B view with its representations from the U-I and B-I views. We construct the same pairs for bundles. The reconstructed U-B view thus serves as the common reference. Each same-entity pair is a positive pair, while other entities of the same type provide in-batch negatives. For every recommendation minibatch, we remove duplicate user IDs and duplicate positive-bundle IDs to form the entity sets $\mathcal S_U$ and $\mathcal S_B$, ensuring that an entity is not repeated as its own in-batch negative.

For two equally sized representation sequences $\mathbf H=(\mathbf h_j)_{j=1}^{n}$ and $\mathbf G=(\mathbf g_j)_{j=1}^{n}$, we define the directional InfoNCE objective \cite{oord2018cpc} as
\begin{equation}
\mathcal I_\tau(\mathbf H,\mathbf G)
=-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}
\log
\frac{\exp\!\left(\operatorname{sim}(\mathbf h_j,\mathbf g_j)/\tau\right)}
{\sum_{k=1}^{n}
\exp\!\left(\operatorname{sim}(\mathbf h_j,\mathbf g_k)/\tau\right)}.
\tag{13}
\end{equation}
The temperature $\tau>0$ controls sensitivity to similarity differences. For each anchor $\mathbf h_j$, its counterpart $\mathbf g_j$ is the positive candidate, and all representations in $\mathbf G$, including the positive, form the denominator. Let $\mathbf E_r^v=(\mathbf e_q^v)_{q\in\mathcal S_r}$ for $r\in\{U,B\}$, with the same entity ordering across views. The pre-fusion objective is
\begin{equation}
\mathcal L_{\mathrm A}
=\sum_{r\in\{U,B\}}
\sum_{v\in\{\mathrm{UI},\mathrm{BI}\}}
\mathcal I_\tau\!\left(\mathbf E_r^{\mathrm{UB}},\mathbf E_r^v\right).
\tag{14}
\end{equation}
Equation (14) encourages agreement for the same user or bundle across U-B--U-I and U-B--B-I pairs while preserving discrimination between entities. A user-side or bundle-side term is omitted when the corresponding entity set contains fewer than two members and therefore provides no in-batch negatives. The U-B view is the common anchor in constructing the objective, and gradients update both representations in each pair. This alignment allows information from the U-I and B-I views to support bundle preference learning when U-B feedback is sparse.

\subsection{Prediction and Model Optimization}

\textbf{View fusion and prediction.} Following MultiCBR \cite{ma2024multicbr}, we adopt its fixed view coefficients to combine the three view-specific representations into a single user representation and a single bundle representation:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\mathbf e_u
&=\omega_1\mathbf e_u^{\mathrm{UB}}
+\omega_2\mathbf e_u^{\mathrm{UI}}
+\omega_3\mathbf e_u^{\mathrm{BI}},\\
\mathbf e_b
&=\omega_1\mathbf e_b^{\mathrm{UB}}
+\omega_2\mathbf e_b^{\mathrm{UI}}
+\omega_3\mathbf e_b^{\mathrm{BI}}.
\end{aligned}
\tag{15}
\end{equation}
Here, $\omega_1$, $\omega_2$, and $\omega_3$ are fixed coefficients for the U-B, U-I, and B-I views, respectively. The same coefficients are used for users and bundles; they are nonnegative and satisfy $\omega_1+\omega_2+\omega_3=1$. The inner product of the fused representations gives the preference score:
\begin{equation}
\hat y_{ub}=\mathbf e_u^\top\mathbf e_b.
\tag{16}
\end{equation}

\textbf{Recommendation objectives.} Let $\mathcal Q$ be a minibatch of $m$ ranking triplets, indexed as $(u_j,b_j^+,b_j^-)$ for $j=1,\ldots,m$. Here, $x_{u_jb_j^+}=1$ and $b_j^-\in\mathcal B\setminus\mathcal N_{u_j}$. Let $\mathbf E_U=(\mathbf e_{u_j})_{j=1}^{m}$ and $\mathbf E_B=(\mathbf e_{b_j^+})_{j=1}^{m}$ denote their fused representation sequences. Following MultiCBR \cite{ma2024multicbr}, the fused-space self-contrastive regularizer is
\begin{equation}
\mathcal L_{\mathrm C}
=\frac{1}{2}\left[
\mathcal I_\tau(\mathbf E_U,\mathbf E_U)
+\mathcal I_\tau(\mathbf E_B,\mathbf E_B)
\right].
\tag{17}
\end{equation}
For each occurrence in the minibatch, the numerator pairs its fused representation with itself, while all minibatch occurrences of the same entity type appear in the denominator. This inherited objective regularizes separation in the fused representation space, whereas $\mathcal L_{\mathrm A}$ explicitly connects different relation views before fusion. The BPR loss \cite{rendle2009bpr} is
\begin{equation}
\mathcal L_{\mathrm{BPR}}
=-\frac{1}{m}
\sum_{j=1}^{m}
\log\sigma\!\left(\hat y_{u_jb_j^+}-\hat y_{u_jb_j^-}\right).
\tag{18}
\end{equation}
Here, $\sigma$ is the sigmoid function. We optimize BPR together with the two regularization objectives:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal L_{\mathrm R}
=\mathcal L_{\mathrm{BPR}}
+\lambda_a\mathcal L_{\mathrm A}
+\lambda_c\mathcal L_{\mathrm C}
+\lambda_r\|\Theta\|_2^2.
\tag{19}
\end{equation}
Here, $\Theta$ comprises the trainable initial user, bundle, and item embeddings of the recommendation backbone, and $\lambda_a,\lambda_c,\lambda_r\geq0$ balance the loss terms. Ranking examples continue to come from $\mathbf X$, even when their edges are not selected for $\widetilde{\mathbf X}$. Graph reconstruction therefore filters propagation connections while preserving the observed feedback used for ranking.

\textbf{Training.} After offline U-B pretraining, each training epoch contains three stages. First, we update the denoiser $f_\theta$ for one pass over $\mathcal U_+$ using $\mathcal L_{\mathrm D}$. Second, the updated denoiser refines every active user's historical preference, scores the observed U-B interactions, and refreshes $\widetilde{\mathbf X}$ through the Top-K selection in Eq. (11). Third, we hold $\widetilde{\mathbf X}$ fixed and update the recommendation parameters $\Theta$ on ranking minibatches using $\mathcal L_{\mathrm R}$. The denoiser and recommendation backbone use separate optimizers. Because Top-K edge selection is discrete, gradients from $\mathcal L_{\mathrm R}$ do not propagate through $\widetilde{\mathbf X}$ to $f_\theta$.

\textbf{Inference.} Using the reconstructed graph associated with the selected model, we compute and cache the three view-specific representations and their fused user and bundle embeddings. For each user, we rank candidate bundles by Eq. (16) after excluding bundles observed in the training data. Graph reconstruction is performed before scoring; the denoiser is not run separately for each candidate bundle.

\subsection{Computational Complexity}

Let $n_+=|\mathcal U_+|$ and $h_{\max}=\max_{u\in\mathcal U_+}|\mathcal N_u|$. Let $M=|\mathcal E_{\mathrm{UB}}|$ be the number of observed U-B edges, and let $E=|\widetilde{\mathcal E}_{\mathrm{UB}}|+|\mathcal E_{\mathrm{UI}}|+|\mathcal E_{\mathrm{BI}}|$ be the total number of edges used for graph propagation. We use $Q$ and $m$ to denote the number and size of recommendation minibatches, respectively. With a fixed-depth denoiser whose hidden widths scale with $d$, one evaluation costs $C_f=O(d^2)$. The time complexity of one training epoch is

\begin{equation}
O\!\left(
\begin{aligned}
&(T+1)n_+C_f\\
&+n_+h_{\max}\!\left[d+\log(1+\kappa)\right]\\
&+Q\bigl(LEd+m^2d\bigr)
\end{aligned}
\right).
\tag{20}
\end{equation}
The three terms correspond to denoiser training and refinement, history processing and bounded-heap Top-K graph reconstruction, and recommendation learning, respectively. Offline LightGCN pretraining requires $O(L_pMd)$ per propagation pass. Since $|\widetilde{\mathcal E}_{\mathrm{UB}}|\leq M$, reconstruction does not densify the U-B graph.

For space complexity, let $V=|\mathcal U|+|\mathcal B|+|\mathcal I|$, let $b_d$ be the maximum denoising or reconstruction batch size, and let $P_\theta$ be the denoiser parameter count. The peak training space is
\begin{equation}
O\!\left(
\begin{aligned}
&(L+1)Vd+P_\theta+(|\mathcal U|+|\mathcal B|)d\\
&+E+M+m^2+b_dh_{\max}d
\end{aligned}
\right).
\tag{21}
\end{equation}
At inference, constructing the cached graph representations costs $O(LEd)$. Scoring one user-bundle pair costs $O(d)$, and scoring all candidate bundles for one user costs $O(|\mathcal B|d)$.


\section{Experiments}

In this section, we evaluate ReCoBR against state-of-the-art bundle
recommendation methods on three public datasets.

\subsection{Experimental Settings}


\paragraph{Datasets.}
To evaluate ReCoBR, we conduct experiments on three public
datasets: \textbf{Youshu}~\cite{chen2019dam},
\textbf{iFashion}~\cite{chen2019pog}, and
\textbf{NetEase}~\cite{cao2017efm}.
Table~\ref{tab:datasets} summarizes their statistics.
For a fair comparison, we use the same data splits and
full-ranking evaluation protocol as MultiCBR~\cite{ma2024multicbr}.
Performance is measured using Recall@$K$ and
NDCG@$K$~\cite{wang2019ngcf} for $K\in\{20,40\}$ under
full-ranking evaluation, with training bundles excluded from
the candidates.

\paragraph{Baselines.}
We compare ReCoBR with ten representative recommendation
methods. \textbf{LightGCN}~\cite{he2020lightgcn} learns collaborative
representations through neighborhood aggregation.
\textbf{SGL}~\cite{wu2021sgl} applies contrastive learning to augmented
graphs. \textbf{XSimGCL}~\cite{yu2024xsimgcl} uses noise-based embedding
augmentation, while \textbf{LightGCL}~\cite{cai2023lightgcl} constructs
contrastive views using singular value decomposition.
These four methods treat bundles as items and use only U-B
interactions. \textbf{BGCN}~\cite{chang2020bgcn} models bundle- and item-level
preferences through graph convolution, while
\textbf{MIDGN}~\cite{zhao2022midgn} disentangles global and local intents.
\textbf{CrossCBR}~\cite{ma2022crosscbr} aligns bundle and item views
through contrastive learning.
\textbf{MultiCBR}~\cite{ma2024multicbr} fuses three relation views before
contrastive learning. \textbf{DCDL}~\cite{chen2025dcdl} combines
dual-view modeling with diffusion learning to refine user
representations and enhance recommendation diversity.
\textbf{EpicCBR}~\cite{li2026epiccbr} combines item-relation
enhancement with contrastive learning.

\paragraph{Parameter Settings.}
All experiments are implemented on a GeForce RTX 3090 GPU.
For ReCoBR, we use 64-dimensional embeddings with
Xavier~\cite{glorot2010xavier} initialization and two propagation
layers. Recommendation training uses
Adam~\cite{kingma2015adam} with learning rate $10^{-3}$,
batch size 2048, and one negative bundle per positive
interaction; $\tau=0.20$ and $\lambda_r=10^{-5}$.
The denoiser is a two-layer MLP with hidden size 128,
timestep embedding size 16, and dropout 0.1, trained with
batch size 1024, learning rate $10^{-3}$, and weight decay
$10^{-5}$.
The minimum noise variance is fixed at $\beta_1=10^{-4}$.

For sensitivity analysis, we vary $\beta_T$ in
$\{0.005,\allowbreak 0.01,\allowbreak 0.02,\allowbreak 0.04,\allowbreak 0.06,\allowbreak 0.08\}$, $\kappa$ in
$\{1,\allowbreak 3,\allowbreak 5,\allowbreak 7,\allowbreak 9,\allowbreak 11,\allowbreak 13,\allowbreak 15,\allowbreak 17,\allowbreak 19\}$, $\lambda_a$ in
$\{0,\allowbreak 0.001,\allowbreak 0.003,\allowbreak 0.005,\allowbreak 0.007,\allowbreak 0.009\}$,
$\lambda_{\mathrm{set}}$ in
$\{0,\allowbreak 0.005,\allowbreak 0.01,\allowbreak 0.02,\allowbreak 0.04,\allowbreak 0.05,\allowbreak 0.10,\allowbreak 0.20\}$, and $\lambda_c$ in
$\{0,\allowbreak 0.01,\allowbreak 0.02,\allowbreak 0.04,\allowbreak 0.05,\allowbreak 0.10,\allowbreak 0.20,\allowbreak 0.40\}$.
For the baselines, we tune hyperparameters within the ranges
specified in their original papers.




\subsection{Overall Performance}

Table~\ref{tab:overall} compares ReCoBR with ten baselines.
We draw the following observations:
\begin{itemize}
\item ReCoBR achieves the best results across all datasets and
metrics. Its Recall@20 improves over the strongest baseline by
2.17\%, 2.94\%, and 4.60\% on Youshu, iFashion, and NetEase,
respectively. The largest relative NDCG gains occur on iFashion. One possible explanation is that iFashion has more U-B interactions
per user, which may help ReCoBR select useful connections for graph
propagation.
\item SGL, XSimGCL, and LightGCL outperform LightGCN across all
datasets and metrics. On iFashion, they also exceed BGCN and
MIDGN despite using only U-B interactions. These results suggest that effective U-B preference learning remains
important even when auxiliary item-level information is available.
\item ReCoBR also outperforms CrossCBR, MultiCBR,
DCDL, and EpicCBR, which model bundle preferences and
integrate information across views. ReCoBR further considers
which U-B connections should guide learning across views, bringing interaction refinement
and auxiliary-information integration into one framework.
ReCoBR's consistent gains across all three datasets support
the value of diffusion-driven graph reconstruction in
uncovering informative U-B preference signals for cross-view
learning.
\end{itemize}


\subsection{Ablation Study}

Table~\ref{tab:ablation} compares ReCoBR with five variants:
\textbf{w/o Graph Recon.} restores the original U-B graph;
\textbf{w/o SetLoss} removes the bundle-set consistency loss;
\textbf{w/o Pre-fusion CL} and \textbf{w/o Post-fusion CL}
remove their respective contrastive objectives; and
\textbf{w/o All CL} removes both. Graph reconstruction
improves ranking performance, particularly on iFashion,
supporting preference-guided selection of propagation
connections. SetLoss yields further gains, reinforcing the
value of consistency with historical bundle preferences.
Removing pre-fusion learning also degrades performance,
highlighting the value of connecting U-B preferences with
auxiliary views before fusion. The larger losses without
post-fusion or all contrastive learning emphasize the
importance of representation regularization. Overall, ReCoBR
outperforms all variants, demonstrating the effectiveness of
jointly refining U-B preference signals and integrating
auxiliary information for bundle recommendation.

\subsection{In-depth Analysis}




\paragraph{Interaction Selection Strategies.}
On iFashion, we compare ReCoBR with the original graph
(\textbf{Original UB}) and three filtering strategies: random
selection (\textbf{Random-K}), bundle popularity
(\textbf{Popularity-K}), and pretrained user-bundle similarity
(\textbf{Pretrain-Sim}). All filtering strategies select from
users' training histories under the same per-user retention
limit $\kappa$. As shown in
Figure~\ref{fig:interaction-selection}, ReCoBR consistently
performs best. Random-K can improve over Original UB but
remains inferior to ReCoBR, indicating that selecting
appropriate connections matters beyond reducing their number.
Popularity-K's weak NDCG suggests that global popularity is
insufficient for personalized ranking. Pretrain-Sim performs worse than Random-K, suggesting that
pretrained user-bundle similarity alone may not identify the most
useful connections for graph propagation. Together, these results suggest that preference-guided selection is
more effective than random, popularity-based, or
pretrained-similarity-based selection.

\paragraph{Robustness to Noisy U-B Interactions.}
We compare ReCoBR with CrossCBR and MultiCBR using the same

\subsection{Hyperparameter Sensitivity}

Figure~\ref{fig:hyperparameter} reports ReCoBR's sensitivity
to five key hyperparameters. We vary one parameter at a time
while keeping the others fixed. For $\beta_T$, intermediate
noise levels yield better performance than the extremes,
suggesting that preference recovery benefits from moderate
perturbation without excessively disrupting historical
information. For $\kappa$, iFashion favors smaller retained
neighborhoods, whereas NetEase benefits from retaining more
connections. This difference highlights the need to balance
neighborhood coverage with preference relevance when selecting
propagation connections. Nonzero $\lambda_{\mathrm{set}}$
weights improve performance on Youshu, while iFashion and
NetEase show only small, nonmonotonic changes. Increasing
this weight does not consistently yield further gains.
Moderate nonzero values of $\lambda_a$ and $\lambda_c$ improve
performance, while larger weights can reduce it. The decline
is especially pronounced for $\lambda_c$ on Youshu and
NetEase, suggesting the need to balance contrastive
regularization with the ranking objective.



\section{Conclusion}

In this paper, we proposed ReCoBR to address noisy and sparse U-B
feedback in bundle recommendation. ReCoBR refined users' historical
bundle preferences through user-conditioned diffusion with a
bundle-set consistency loss. The refined preferences guided the
selection of observed U-B connections to reconstruct the graph for
propagation. Cross-view contrastive learning aligned user and bundle
representations from the reconstructed U-B view with those from the
U-I and B-I views. Experiments on three public datasets showed that
ReCoBR outperformed the compared baselines, and ablation results
supported the effectiveness of its main components.
