\begin{abstract}
Bundle recommendation (BR) aims to match users with collections of items that suit their preferences. Existing graph-based methods learn bundle preferences primarily from user-bundle (U-B) interactions, while incorporating user-item (U-I) interactions and bundle-item (B-I) affiliations as auxiliary information. Despite their effectiveness, limitations in the observed U-B interactions still pose two challenges: (1) noise in the observed U-B graph can introduce misleading preference signals during graph propagation; (2) sparse U-B feedback hinders accurate modeling of users' bundle preferences. To address these challenges, we propose ReCoBR. ReCoBR reduces the impact of noisy interactions through Pretraining-Based Diffusion-Driven U-B Graph Reconstruction (PDGR) and improves preference learning under sparse feedback through Cross-View Contrastive Learning (CCL). In PDGR, we first pretrain user and bundle embeddings on the observed U-B graph. We construct users' historical preference representations from the bundle embeddings and denoise the preference representations with a user-conditioned diffusion model. We then use the denoised representations to filter observed U-B interactions and reconstruct the U-B graph. Next, CCL applies contrastive learning between the reconstructed U-B view and each auxiliary view before representation fusion, enabling auxiliary information to better support bundle preference learning for sparse users. Experiments on three public datasets show that ReCoBR outperforms the baseline methods.
\end{abstract}


\section{Introduction}

Bundle recommendation (BR) aims to match users with collections of related items, such as music playlists and fashion outfits~\cite{chen2019dam,ma2022crosscbr}. BR helps users discover suitable collections while creating additional sales opportunities for platforms~\cite{ma2022crosscbr,ma2024multicbr}. To learn users' bundle preferences, BR methods commonly use three types of relations~\cite{ma2022crosscbr,ma2024multicbr}: user-bundle (U-B) interactions reflect users' choices of bundles; user-item (U-I) interactions provide information about their interests in individual items; and bundle-item (B-I) affiliations describe which items belong to each bundle. Together, these relations provide information for learning users' bundle preferences.

Early BR methods explored how to incorporate item-level information into bundle preference learning. For example, DAM jointly models U-B and U-I interactions, using attention to construct bundle representations from item embeddings~\cite{chen2019dam}. To capture higher-order relations among users, bundles, and items, BGCN introduces graph propagation in the bundle and item views~\cite{chang2020bgcn}. BGCN also uses B-I affiliations to aggregate item information into bundle representations. Building on multi-view representation learning, subsequent studies have focused on strengthening information exchange and integration across views. For instance, CrossCBR applies contrastive learning to align representations of the same user (bundle) across the bundle and item views~\cite{ma2022crosscbr}. Meanwhile, DGMAE uses knowledge distillation to transfer information from first- and higher-order U-B relations to the item view~\cite{ren2023dgmae}. More recently, MultiCBR separately learns representations from the U-B, U-I, and B-I views, fuses these representations for recommendation, and applies contrastive learning after fusion~\cite{ma2024multicbr}. Recent studies have also investigated diffusion models to improve user and bundle representations for recommendation. Specifically, DCDL applies diffusion learning to user representations within a dual-view framework to improve recommendation diversity~\cite{chen2025dcdl}. In addition, RDiffBR uses residual diffusion to adapt bundle representations to changes in bundle composition~\cite{zhang2026rdiffbr}.

Although these methods differ in their modeling strategies, they learn users' bundle preferences primarily from observed U-B interactions. However, noise and sparsity in the observed U-B interactions remain significant obstacles to accurately modeling users' bundle preferences. For example, accidental clicks may be recorded as positive interactions even when they do not reflect users' actual preferences~\cite{jiang2026cafu}. During graph propagation, misleading preference signals from noisy U-B interactions can spread through neighborhood aggregation~\cite{wu2021sgl}. Meanwhile, many users have interacted with only a few bundles, leaving limited historical information for learning their preferences~\cite{chang2020bgcn}. For these users, U-I interactions and B-I affiliations can provide additional information, but effectively using this information for bundle preference learning remains difficult. Based on the above analysis, we identify the following two challenges.

\textbf{Challenge 1: Denoising U-B Feedback While Preserving Preference Information.} The observed U-B graph does not indicate which connections are noisy, making it difficult to decide which ones to retain for graph propagation. Retaining unreliable connections may allow misleading preference signals to spread through neighborhood aggregation. However, removing too many connections can also discard valuable information about users' historical bundle choices. The challenge is to develop an effective mechanism for assessing the reliability of observed U-B interactions to guide graph denoising.

\textbf{Challenge 2: Cross-View Collaboration under Sparse U-B Feedback.} Sparse U-B interactions hinder accurate modeling of users' bundle preferences. To alleviate U-B sparsity, it is important to effectively use the additional information provided by U-I interactions and B-I affiliations. Existing three-view methods such as MultiCBR separately learn representations through graph propagation in each view and integrate these representations through weighted fusion~\cite{ma2024multicbr}. However, simple weighted fusion lacks direct cross-view constraints that can provide additional supervision for preference learning under sparse U-B feedback. The challenge is to make more effective use of information from the U-I and B-I views to improve bundle preference learning for sparse users.

To address these challenges, we propose \textbf{Reconstruction and Contrastive Learning for Bundle Recommendation (ReCoBR)}, which comprises Pretraining-Based Diffusion-Driven U-B Graph Reconstruction (PDGR) and Cross-View Contrastive Learning (CCL). PDGR provides a diffusion-driven mechanism to assess the reliability of observed U-B interactions and guide graph denoising. Specifically, we first pretrain user and bundle embeddings on the observed U-B graph using LightGCN~\cite{he2020lightgcn}. We then construct each user's preference representation by averaging the pretrained embeddings of their historically interacted bundles. The resulting preference representations are denoised by a diffusion model conditioned on the pretrained user embeddings. During denoising, we introduce a bundle-set consistency loss (SetLoss) to preserve useful preference information by aligning the denoised preferences with users' historical bundle embeddings. Finally, we score the observed U-B interactions using the denoised preferences and retain high-scoring connections to reconstruct the graph for propagation. Building on the reconstructed U-B graph, CCL offers additional supervision for preference learning under sparse U-B feedback. Specifically, we apply contrastive learning before fusion to align representations of the same user (bundle) between the reconstructed U-B view and each auxiliary view. These cross-view constraints allow auxiliary information to directly support the learning of bundle preferences. We then fuse the representations for recommendation while retaining the post-fusion contrastive objective~\cite{ma2024multicbr}. Overall, ReCoBR establishes a unified framework that integrates diffusion and cross-view contrastive learning to effectively address noise and sparsity in U-B interactions.
Our main contributions are summarized as follows:

\begin{enumerate}
\item We propose a bundle recommendation framework, ReCoBR, which uses diffusion-driven U-B graph reconstruction based on pretrained embeddings to reduce the impact of noisy interactions.
\item We design cross-view contrastive learning based on the reconstructed U-B view, enabling more effective use of auxiliary information to improve bundle preference learning for sparse users.
\item Experiments on three public datasets demonstrate the effectiveness of ReCoBR.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}

\subsection{Bundle Recommendation}

Bundle recommendation includes both ranking predefined bundles and constructing new item collections~\cite{sun2024survey}. For bundle ranking, early methods connect users' bundle choices with their item interests through factorization models~\cite{cao2017efm} or attention-based aggregation in DAM~\cite{chen2019dam}. Graph-based methods further capture higher-order relations among users, bundles, and items. For example, BGCN propagates information at both the bundle and item levels to learn user and bundle representations~\cite{chang2020bgcn}.

Later studies seek richer preference representations by modeling user intents and bundle structure or by exploiting additional collaborative information. MIDGN disentangles user intents across and within bundles~\cite{zhao2022midgn}, while BundleGT models item associations within bundles using a hierarchical graph Transformer~\cite{wei2023bundlegt}. To strengthen item-level representations, DGMAE transfers knowledge of first- and higher-order U-B relations to the item view through knowledge distillation~\cite{ren2023dgmae}. EBRec also uses bundle--user--item correlations and augmented U-I interactions to enrich item-level bundle representations~\cite{du2025ebrec}. EpicCBR mines item relations from U-I interactions and B-I affiliations to enhance the U-I view, then uses multi-view contrastive learning to improve user and bundle representations~\cite{li2026epiccbr}.

\subsection{Denoising and Diffusion for Recommendation}

Denoising methods seek to reduce the influence of noisy implicit feedback on preference learning. ADT filters or downweights high-loss interactions~\cite{wang2021adt}, while DeCA uses agreement between model predictions to guide denoising~\cite{wang2022deca}. At the representation level, CAFU reduces noise through filtered uniformity and improves learning for sparse users through constrained alignment~\cite{jiang2026cafu}.

Diffusion models offer a different approach by learning to reconstruct data or representations corrupted by added noise~\cite{ho2020ddpm}. DiffRec applies this process to user interaction vectors~\cite{wang2023diffrec}, whereas DiffuRec reconstructs target-item embeddings for sequential recommendation~\cite{li2023diffurec}. To improve robustness against noisy feedback, DDRM applies diffusion denoising to user and item embeddings~\cite{zhao2024ddrm}.

In bundle recommendation, diffusion has been explored to improve diversity and robustness. DCDL denoises user representations with guidance from fused user features to improve recommendation diversity~\cite{chen2025dcdl}. RDiffBR instead focuses on changes in bundle composition, using residual diffusion to adapt item-level bundle representations~\cite{zhang2026rdiffbr}. ReCoBR applies diffusion to historical preference representations and uses the denoised preferences to filter observed U-B interactions for graph propagation.

\subsection{Contrastive Learning for Recommendation}

Contrastive learning provides additional supervision by bringing positive pairs closer and pushing negative pairs apart in the representation space~\cite{chen2020simclr}. In graph-based recommendation, SGL constructs contrastive views through random graph augmentations, including node and edge dropout~\cite{wu2021sgl}. To make view construction adaptive, AdaGCL uses trainable graph generation and denoising models~\cite{jiang2023adagcl}. For intent modeling, BIGCF applies contrastive regularization to both interaction and intent representations~\cite{zhang2024bigcf}.

In bundle recommendation, CrossCBR aligns representations of the same user (bundle) across the bundle and item views before fusion~\cite{ma2022crosscbr}. In contrast, MultiCBR applies contrastive learning to the fused representations of the U-B, U-I, and B-I views~\cite{ma2024multicbr}. ReCoBR adds contrastive learning between the reconstructed U-B view and each auxiliary view before fusion, while retaining the post-fusion contrastive objective.






\section{Methodology}\label{sec:methodology}

\subsection{Task Definition and Framework Overview}

Let $\mathcal U$, $\mathcal B$, and $\mathcal I$ denote the sets of users, predefined bundles, and items, respectively. The available training data comprise three binary matrices: user-bundle interactions $\mathbf X\in\{0,1\}^{|\mathcal U|\times|\mathcal B|}$, user-item interactions $\mathbf Y\in\{0,1\}^{|\mathcal U|\times|\mathcal I|}$, and bundle-item affiliations $\mathbf Z\in\{0,1\}^{|\mathcal B|\times|\mathcal I|}$. In $\mathbf X$ and $\mathbf Y$, an entry equal to one denotes an observed interaction; in $\mathbf Z$, it denotes bundle membership. Given these relations, our task is to estimate a preference score $\hat y_{ub}$ for each unobserved user-bundle pair and rank candidate bundles for each user.

ReCoBR consists of two modules: PDGR and CCL. To reduce the influence of noisy interactions, PDGR uses a user-conditioned diffusion model to refine historical preference representations constructed from pretrained bundle embeddings. These refined preferences guide U-B interaction filtering and graph reconstruction. Building on the reconstructed graph, CCL introduces cross-view constraints before representation fusion, allowing auxiliary information to support bundle preference learning for sparse users. The fused user and bundle representations are then used for recommendation.

We distinguish the observed matrix $\mathbf X$ from the reconstructed matrix $\widetilde{\mathbf X}$ throughout this section. The former defines users' training histories and ranking examples; the latter determines the U-B connections used for recommendation graph propagation.

\subsection{Pretraining-Based Diffusion-Driven U-B Graph Reconstruction (PDGR)}\label{sec:graph-reconstruction}

PDGR assesses the reliability of observed U-B interactions through preference-based scoring. We construct historical preference representations from pretrained embeddings and train a user-conditioned denoiser with a bundle-set consistency constraint. The refined preferences are then used to score observed interactions and retain high-scoring connections for graph propagation.

\subsubsection{Pretraining and Historical Preference Construction}

To capture collaborative information from U-B interactions, we pretrain LightGCN \cite{he2020lightgcn} on the observed U-B graph using Bayesian Personalized Ranking (BPR) \cite{rendle2009bpr}. Let $\mathbf h_u^{(0)},\mathbf h_b^{(0)}\in\mathbb R^d$ denote the trainable initial embeddings. The observed neighbors of user $u$ and bundle $b$ are $\mathcal N_u=\{b\in\mathcal B:x_{ub}=1\}$ and $\mathcal N_b=\{u\in\mathcal U:x_{ub}=1\}$, respectively. LightGCN propagates information between users and bundles as follows:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\mathbf h_u^{(\ell+1)}
&=\sum_{b\in\mathcal N_u}
\frac{\mathbf h_b^{(\ell)}}
{\sqrt{|\mathcal N_u||\mathcal N_b|}},\\
\mathbf h_b^{(\ell+1)}
&=\sum_{u\in\mathcal N_b}
\frac{\mathbf h_u^{(\ell)}}
{\sqrt{|\mathcal N_b||\mathcal N_u|}}.
\end{aligned}
\tag{1}
\end{equation}
After $L_p$ propagation layers, we combine the initial and propagated embeddings to obtain the pretrained user and bundle embeddings:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf e_u^0=\sum_{\ell=0}^{L_p}\eta_\ell\mathbf h_u^{(\ell)},
\qquad
\mathbf e_b^0=\sum_{\ell=0}^{L_p}\eta_\ell\mathbf h_b^{(\ell)}.
\tag{2}
\end{equation}
Here, $\eta_\ell$ is the fixed weight assigned to layer $\ell$, with $\eta_\ell\geq0$ and $\sum_{\ell=0}^{L_p}\eta_\ell=1$.
The pretrained embeddings remain fixed throughout denoiser and recommendation training. We use $\mathbf e_b^0$ to construct historical preference representations and score observed interactions, while $\mathbf e_u^0$ provides the user condition for denoising.

We construct historical preference representations for users with observed U-B interactions, denoted by $\mathcal U_+=\{u\in\mathcal U:|\mathcal N_u|>0\}$. For each $u\in\mathcal U_+$, we average the pretrained embeddings of their historically interacted bundles:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf p_{u,0}
=\frac{1}{|\mathcal N_u|}
\sum_{b\in\mathcal N_u}\mathbf e_b^0.
\tag{3}
\end{equation}
The resulting vector $\mathbf p_{u,0}$ summarizes the user's historical bundle preferences. Mean pooling maps histories of different lengths to a $d$-dimensional representation. The subscript $0$ denotes the preference representation before Gaussian noise is added.

\subsubsection{User-Conditioned Diffusion Denoising}

We train the denoiser to recover historical preference representations from inputs with different noise levels. Following the Gaussian diffusion formulation \cite{ho2020ddpm,wang2023diffrec}, let $T$ be the number of diffusion steps and $\beta_t\in(0,1)$ the noise variance at step $t$. We use a linear variance schedule, with $\alpha_t=1-\beta_t$ and $\bar\alpha_t=\prod_{s=1}^{t}\alpha_s$. At step $t$, the noisy preference representation is
\begin{equation}
\mathbf p_{u,t}
=\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,\mathbf p_{u,0}
+\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\boldsymbol\epsilon,
\qquad
\boldsymbol\epsilon\sim\mathcal N(\mathbf 0,\mathbf I_d).
\tag{4}
\end{equation}
Here, $\boldsymbol\epsilon\in\mathbb R^d$ is standard Gaussian noise, and $\mathbf I_d$ is the $d$-dimensional identity matrix. The coefficient $\bar\alpha_t$ controls the contribution of the historical preference representation at step $t$.

To personalize denoising, we condition the model on the pretrained user embedding $\mathbf e_u^0$. Following DiffRec \cite{wang2023diffrec}, we use a multilayer perceptron (MLP) as the denoising network. Our denoiser $f_\theta$, parameterized by $\theta$, predicts the historical preference representation from the noisy input:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\widehat{\mathbf p}_{u,0}
&=f_\theta(\mathbf p_{u,t},\mathbf e_u^0,t)\\
&=\operatorname{MLP}_\theta\bigl([\mathbf p_{u,t};\mathbf e_u^0;
\mathbf p_{u,t}\odot\mathbf e_u^0;\\
&\hspace{20mm}|\mathbf p_{u,t}-\mathbf e_u^0|;\boldsymbol\psi(t)]\bigr).
\end{aligned}
\tag{5}
\end{equation}
Here, $[\cdot;\cdot]$ denotes concatenation and $\odot$ denotes element-wise multiplication. The input includes the noisy preference, the pretrained user embedding, their element-wise product, and their absolute difference. These features allow the network to model the relationship between the noisy preference and the user condition. We further include a sinusoidal timestep embedding $\boldsymbol\psi(t)\in\mathbb R^{d_t}$ to indicate the noise level. The MLP maps the resulting $(4d+d_t)$-dimensional input to a $d$-dimensional preference representation.

During training, we uniformly sample a user $u\in\mathcal U_+$ and a timestep $t\in\{1,\ldots,T\}$. We then sample Gaussian noise to construct $\mathbf p_{u,t}$ as in Eq. (4). The mean squared error (MSE) reconstruction loss is
\begin{equation}
\mathcal L_{\mathrm{mse}}
=\mathbb E_{u,t,\boldsymbol\epsilon}
\left[
\frac{1}{d}
\left\|\widehat{\mathbf p}_{u,0}-\mathbf p_{u,0}\right\|_2^2
\right].
\tag{6}
\end{equation}
Minimizing this loss trains the denoiser to recover $\mathbf p_{u,0}$ from $\mathbf p_{u,t}$, with $\mathbf e_u^0$ providing user-specific information.

\subsubsection{Bundle-Set Consistency in Denoising}

The reconstruction loss in Eq. (6) supervises recovery of the pooled historical preference vector. Graph reconstruction, however, requires scoring each historical bundle against the denoised preference. We therefore introduce a bundle-set consistency loss (SetLoss) to preserve useful preference information from the user's historical bundles.

Let $\cos(\mathbf a,\mathbf b)=\mathbf a^\top\mathbf b/(\|\mathbf a\|_2\|\mathbf b\|_2)$ denote cosine similarity, with a small numerical safeguard for zero norms. SetLoss encourages consistency between the denoised preference and the user's historical bundle embeddings:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal L_{\mathrm{set}}
=\mathbb E_{u,t,\boldsymbol\epsilon}
\left[
\frac{1}{|\mathcal N_u|}
\sum_{b\in\mathcal N_u}
\left(1-\cos
\left(\widehat{\mathbf p}_{u,0},\mathbf e_b^0\right)\right)
\right].
\tag{7}
\end{equation}
SetLoss supplements the pooled-vector reconstruction with consistency constraints between the denoised preference and individual historical bundle embeddings. Using the same cosine similarity for SetLoss and subsequent interaction scoring connects preference denoising with graph reconstruction. Averaging over the history normalizes each user's loss by their number of observed bundles. We jointly optimize the two losses:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal L_{\mathrm D}
=\mathcal L_{\mathrm{mse}}
+\lambda_{\mathrm{set}}\mathcal L_{\mathrm{set}},
\tag{8}
\end{equation}
where $\lambda_{\mathrm{set}}\geq 0$ controls the contribution of SetLoss.

\subsubsection{Preference Refinement and Graph Reconstruction}

To reconstruct the U-B graph, we apply the current denoiser to each user's historical preference representation. Starting from $\mathbf p_{u,0}$, we repeatedly update the representation over timesteps $T,T-1,\ldots,1$:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\mathbf r_{u,T}&=\mathbf p_{u,0},\\
\mathbf r_{u,t-1}
&=f_\theta(\mathbf r_{u,t},\mathbf e_u^0,t),
\qquad t=T,T-1,\ldots,1,\\
\mathbf p_u^\star&=\mathbf r_{u,0}.
\end{aligned}
\tag{9}
\end{equation}
Here, $\mathbf r_{u,t}$ is the intermediate representation during refinement, and $\mathbf p_u^\star$ is the final refined preference. Each step passes the current representation, the pretrained user embedding, and the timestep to the denoiser.

We then score each observed U-B interaction by the cosine similarity between the refined preference and the corresponding pretrained bundle embedding:
\begin{equation}
s_{ub}
=\cos(\mathbf p_u^\star,\mathbf e_b^0),
\qquad b\in\mathcal N_u.
\tag{10}
\end{equation}
Higher scores indicate a closer match to the user's refined preference. For each user, we retain the highest-scoring $\kappa_u=\min(\kappa,|\mathcal N_u|)$ bundles. The positive integer $\kappa$ specifies the maximum number of retained interactions per user:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\widetilde{\mathcal N}_u
&=\underset{b\in\mathcal N_u}{\operatorname{TopK}}
\left(s_{ub},\kappa_u\right),\\
\widetilde x_{ub}
&=\mathbf 1\!\left[b\in\widetilde{\mathcal N}_u\right].
\end{aligned}
\tag{11}
\end{equation}
Here, $\operatorname{TopK}(s_{ub},\kappa_u)$ returns the set of $\kappa_u$ bundles in $\mathcal N_u$ with the largest scores, and $\mathbf 1[\cdot]$ is the indicator function. Users with at most $\kappa$ observed bundles retain all their connections. The selected interactions form $\widetilde{\mathbf X}$, which defines the reconstructed U-B graph for propagation.

\subsection{Cross-View Contrastive Learning (CCL)}

Building on the reconstructed U-B graph, CCL uses the U-I and B-I views to provide additional supervision for bundle preference learning. We encode the three relation views, then align representations of the same user (bundle) between the reconstructed U-B view and each auxiliary view before fusion. These direct cross-view constraints help auxiliary information support preference learning for sparse users.

\subsubsection{View-Specific Representations}

Following the three-view backbone of MultiCBR \cite{ma2024multicbr}, we construct the U-B, U-I, and B-I propagation graphs from $\widetilde{\mathbf X}$, $\mathbf Y$, and $\mathbf Z$, respectively. The encoders share trainable initial embeddings for the same entity. These embeddings are learned during recommendation training, separately from the fixed pretrained embeddings used by PDGR.

Each encoder applies $L$ layers of symmetrically normalized LightGCN-style propagation \cite{he2020lightgcn}. Within each view, fixed layer weights combine the initial embeddings and the $\ell_2$-normalized propagation outputs. During training, we add small representation perturbations to the propagation outputs and auxiliary item pooling. These perturbations are disabled during evaluation.

\textbf{U-B view.} Propagation on the reconstructed U-B graph directly produces user representations $\mathbf e_u^{\mathrm{UB}}$ and bundle representations $\mathbf e_b^{\mathrm{UB}}$, capturing collaborative information through the retained interactions.

\textbf{U-I view.} Propagation on the U-I graph produces user representations $\mathbf e_u^{\mathrm{UI}}$ and item representations $\mathbf e_i^{\mathrm{UI}}$. To obtain a bundle representation $\mathbf e_b^{\mathrm{UI}}$ in this view, we average the representations of its constituent items according to $\mathbf Z$.

\textbf{B-I view.} Propagation on the B-I graph produces bundle representations $\mathbf e_b^{\mathrm{BI}}$ and item representations $\mathbf e_i^{\mathrm{BI}}$. To obtain a user representation $\mathbf e_u^{\mathrm{BI}}$ in this view, we average the representations of the items that the user has interacted with according to $\mathbf Y$.

The two item-pooling operations are
\begin{equation}
\mathbf e_b^{\mathrm{UI}}
=\frac{1}{|\mathcal I_b|}
\sum_{i\in\mathcal I_b}\mathbf e_i^{\mathrm{UI}},
\qquad
\mathbf e_u^{\mathrm{BI}}
=\frac{1}{|\mathcal I_u|}
\sum_{i\in\mathcal I_u}\mathbf e_i^{\mathrm{BI}},
\tag{12}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal I_b=\{i:z_{bi}=1\}$ contains the items in bundle $b$, and $\mathcal I_u=\{i:y_{ui}=1\}$ contains the items interacted with by user $u$. An empty neighborhood contributes a zero vector.

After graph propagation and item pooling, each user (bundle) has a representation in all three views. We use these $d$-dimensional representations for cross-view contrastive learning before fusing them for recommendation.

\subsubsection{Cross-View Contrastive Objectives}

For each recommendation minibatch, we collect unique users and unique positive bundles into $\mathcal S_U$ and $\mathcal S_B$, respectively. For each user (bundle), we pair the representation from the reconstructed U-B view with its counterpart in each auxiliary view. Representations of the same entity form a positive pair, while those of other entities of the same type provide in-batch negatives. Using unique entities avoids treating repeated IDs as negative samples. We omit the user-side or bundle-side terms if the corresponding set contains fewer than two entities.

For two equally sized representation sequences $\mathbf H=(\mathbf h_j)_{j=1}^{n}$ and $\mathbf G=(\mathbf g_j)_{j=1}^{n}$, we define the directional InfoNCE objective \cite{oord2018cpc} as
\begin{equation}
\mathcal I_\tau(\mathbf H,\mathbf G)
=-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}
\log
\frac{\exp\!\left(\cos(\mathbf h_j,\mathbf g_j)/\tau\right)}
{\sum_{k=1}^{n}
\exp\!\left(\cos(\mathbf h_j,\mathbf g_k)/\tau\right)}.
\tag{13}
\end{equation}
Here, $\tau>0$ is the contrastive temperature. For anchor $\mathbf h_j$, $\mathbf g_j$ is the positive sample, while $\mathbf g_k$ with $k\neq j$ provides a negative sample. Let $\mathbf E_r^v=(\mathbf e_q^v)_{q\in\mathcal S_r}$ for $r\in\{U,B\}$, with the same entity ordering across views. The pre-fusion objective combines U-B--U-I and U-B--B-I pairs for both users and bundles:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal L_{\mathrm A}
=\sum_{r\in\{U,B\}}
\sum_{v\in\{\mathrm{UI},\mathrm{BI}\}}
\mathcal I_\tau\!\left(\mathbf E_r^{\mathrm{UB}},\mathbf E_r^v\right).
\tag{14}
\end{equation}
Minimizing $\mathcal L_{\mathrm A}$ brings representations of the same entity closer across views and separates different entities. The reconstructed U-B view serves as the common anchor, with gradients updating both representations in each pair. Through these constraints, auxiliary information contributes to preference learning before fusion, providing additional supervision for sparse users.

\subsection{Prediction and Model Optimization}

\textbf{View fusion and prediction.} Following the weighted fusion strategy of MultiCBR \cite{ma2024multicbr}, we combine the three view-specific representations using fixed coefficients:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\mathbf e_u
&=\omega_1\mathbf e_u^{\mathrm{UB}}
+\omega_2\mathbf e_u^{\mathrm{UI}}
+\omega_3\mathbf e_u^{\mathrm{BI}},\\
\mathbf e_b
&=\omega_1\mathbf e_b^{\mathrm{UB}}
+\omega_2\mathbf e_b^{\mathrm{UI}}
+\omega_3\mathbf e_b^{\mathrm{BI}}.
\end{aligned}
\tag{15}
\end{equation}
Here, $\omega_1$, $\omega_2$, and $\omega_3$ are fixed coefficients for the U-B, U-I, and B-I views, respectively. The same coefficients are used for users and bundles; they are nonnegative and satisfy $\omega_1+\omega_2+\omega_3=1$. The inner product of the fused representations gives the preference score:
\begin{equation}
\hat y_{ub}=\mathbf e_u^\top\mathbf e_b.
\tag{16}
\end{equation}

\textbf{Recommendation objectives.} Let $\mathcal Q$ be a minibatch of $m$ ranking triplets, indexed as $(u_j,b_j^+,b_j^-)$ for $j=1,\ldots,m$. Here, $x_{u_jb_j^+}=1$ and $b_j^-\in\mathcal B\setminus\mathcal N_{u_j}$. Let $\mathbf E_U=(\mathbf e_{u_j})_{j=1}^{m}$ and $\mathbf E_B=(\mathbf e_{b_j^+})_{j=1}^{m}$ denote their fused representation sequences. We also retain contrastive learning after fusion, following the strategy of MultiCBR \cite{ma2024multicbr}. Our post-fusion contrastive objective is
\begin{equation}
\mathcal L_{\mathrm C}
=\frac{1}{2}\left[
\mathcal I_\tau(\mathbf E_U,\mathbf E_U)
+\mathcal I_\tau(\mathbf E_B,\mathbf E_B)
\right].
\tag{17}
\end{equation}
In Eq. (17), each fused representation is paired with itself, with all representations of the same entity type in the minibatch used for comparison. Repeated users or bundles retain their separate positions in these sequences. This objective encourages separation between different entities in the fused representation space. Together, $\mathcal L_{\mathrm A}$ and $\mathcal L_{\mathrm C}$ constrain view-specific and fused representations, respectively.

For recommendation, the BPR loss \cite{rendle2009bpr} encourages higher scores for observed bundles than for unobserved bundles:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal L_{\mathrm{BPR}}
=-\frac{1}{m}
\sum_{j=1}^{m}
\log\sigma\!\left(\hat y_{u_jb_j^+}-\hat y_{u_jb_j^-}\right).
\tag{18}
\end{equation}
Here, $\sigma$ is the sigmoid function. The overall recommendation objective combines BPR, the two contrastive losses, and embedding regularization:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal L_{\mathrm R}
=\mathcal L_{\mathrm{BPR}}
+\lambda_a\mathcal L_{\mathrm A}
+\lambda_c\mathcal L_{\mathrm C}
+\lambda_r\|\Theta\|_2^2.
\tag{19}
\end{equation}
The parameter set $\Theta$ contains the trainable initial user, bundle, and item embeddings of the recommendation backbone. The coefficients $\lambda_a,\lambda_c,\lambda_r\geq0$ balance the loss terms. We sample ranking triplets using the original interactions in $\mathbf X$.

\textbf{Training.} After offline U-B pretraining, each training epoch proceeds in three stages. First, we update the denoiser $f_\theta$ for one pass over $\mathcal U_+$ using $\mathcal L_{\mathrm D}$. Second, we use the updated denoiser to refine historical preferences and score observed U-B interactions for all users in $\mathcal U_+$. The Top-K selection in Eq. (11) then refreshes $\widetilde{\mathbf X}$. Third, we hold $\widetilde{\mathbf X}$ fixed and update the recommendation parameters $\Theta$ on ranking minibatches using $\mathcal L_{\mathrm R}$. The denoiser and recommendation backbone use separate optimizers. Top-K selection is discrete, so recommendation gradients do not propagate through the reconstructed graph to the denoiser.

\textbf{Inference.} Using the reconstructed graph associated with the selected model, we compute and cache the three view-specific representations and their fused user and bundle embeddings. For each user, we rank candidate bundles by Eq. (16) after excluding bundles observed in the training data. The denoiser is used only during graph reconstruction, before candidate scoring.

\subsection{Computational Complexity}

Each training epoch includes denoiser training, graph reconstruction, and recommendation learning. Let $n_+=|\mathcal U_+|$ be the number of users with observed U-B interactions, and let $h_{\max}=\max_{u\in\mathcal U_+}|\mathcal N_u|$ be the maximum history length. The observed U-B graph has $M=|\mathcal E_{\mathrm{UB}}|$ edges. The three propagation graphs contain $E=|\widetilde{\mathcal E}_{\mathrm{UB}}|+|\mathcal E_{\mathrm{UI}}|+|\mathcal E_{\mathrm{BI}}|$ edges in total. We denote the number and size of recommendation minibatches by $Q$ and $m$, respectively. For a fixed-depth denoiser whose hidden widths scale with $d$, one evaluation costs $C_f=O(d^2)$. The time complexity of one training epoch is

\begin{equation}
O\!\left(
\begin{aligned}
&(T+1)n_+C_f\\
&+n_+h_{\max}\!\left[d+\log(1+\kappa)\right]\\
&+Q\bigl(LEd+m^2d\bigr)
\end{aligned}
\right).
\tag{20}
\end{equation}
The three terms account for denoiser training and refinement, history processing and bounded-heap Top-K graph reconstruction, and recommendation learning, respectively. Offline LightGCN pretraining requires $O(L_pMd)$ per propagation pass. Graph reconstruction retains a subset of the observed interactions, so $|\widetilde{\mathcal E}_{\mathrm{UB}}|\leq M$.

For space complexity, let $V=|\mathcal U|+|\mathcal B|+|\mathcal I|$ denote the total number of entities. Let $b_d$ be the maximum denoising or reconstruction batch size, and let $P_\theta$ be the number of denoiser parameters. The peak training space is
\begin{equation}
O\!\left(
\begin{aligned}
&(L+1)Vd+P_\theta+(|\mathcal U|+|\mathcal B|)d\\
&+E+M+m^2+b_dh_{\max}d
\end{aligned}
\right).
\tag{21}
\end{equation}
At inference, constructing the cached graph representations costs $O(LEd)$. Scoring one user-bundle pair costs $O(d)$, and scoring all candidate bundles for one user costs $O(|\mathcal B|d)$.


\section{Experiments}

In this section, we evaluate ReCoBR against state-of-the-art bundle
recommendation methods on three public datasets.

\subsection{Experimental Settings}


\paragraph{Datasets.}
To evaluate ReCoBR, we conduct experiments on three public
datasets: \textbf{Youshu}~\cite{chen2019dam},
\textbf{iFashion}~\cite{chen2019pog}, and
\textbf{NetEase}~\cite{cao2017efm}.
Table~\ref{tab:datasets} summarizes their statistics.
For a fair comparison, we use the same data splits and
full-ranking evaluation protocol as MultiCBR~\cite{ma2024multicbr}.
Performance is measured using Recall@$K$ and
NDCG@$K$~\cite{wang2019ngcf} for $K\in\{20,40\}$ under
full-ranking evaluation, with training bundles excluded from
the candidates.

\paragraph{Baselines.}
We compare ReCoBR with ten representative recommendation
methods. \textbf{LightGCN}~\cite{he2020lightgcn} learns collaborative
representations through neighborhood aggregation.
\textbf{SGL}~\cite{wu2021sgl} applies contrastive learning to augmented
graphs. \textbf{XSimGCL}~\cite{yu2024xsimgcl} uses noise-based embedding
augmentation, while \textbf{LightGCL}~\cite{cai2023lightgcl} constructs
contrastive views using singular value decomposition.
These four methods treat bundles as items and use only U-B
interactions. \textbf{BGCN}~\cite{chang2020bgcn} models bundle- and item-level
preferences through graph convolution, while
\textbf{MIDGN}~\cite{zhao2022midgn} disentangles global and local intents.
\textbf{CrossCBR}~\cite{ma2022crosscbr} aligns bundle and item views
through contrastive learning.
\textbf{MultiCBR}~\cite{ma2024multicbr} fuses three relation views before
contrastive learning. \textbf{DCDL}~\cite{chen2025dcdl} combines
dual-view modeling with diffusion learning to refine user
representations and enhance recommendation diversity.
\textbf{EpicCBR}~\cite{li2026epiccbr} combines item-relation
enhancement with contrastive learning.

\paragraph{Parameter Settings.}
All experiments are implemented on a GeForce RTX 3090 GPU.
For ReCoBR, we use 64-dimensional embeddings with
Xavier~\cite{glorot2010xavier} initialization and two propagation
layers. Recommendation training uses
Adam~\cite{kingma2015adam} with learning rate $10^{-3}$,
batch size 2048, and one negative bundle per positive
interaction; $\tau=0.20$ and $\lambda_r=10^{-5}$.
The denoiser is a two-layer MLP with hidden size 128,
timestep embedding size 16, and dropout 0.1, trained with
batch size 1024, learning rate $10^{-3}$, and weight decay
$10^{-5}$.
The minimum noise variance is fixed at $\beta_1=10^{-4}$.

For sensitivity analysis, we vary $\beta_T$ in
$\{0.005,\allowbreak 0.01,\allowbreak 0.02,\allowbreak 0.04,\allowbreak 0.06,\allowbreak 0.08\}$, $\kappa$ in
$\{1,\allowbreak 3,\allowbreak 5,\allowbreak 7,\allowbreak 9,\allowbreak 11,\allowbreak 13,\allowbreak 15,\allowbreak 17,\allowbreak 19\}$, $\lambda_a$ in
$\{0,\allowbreak 0.001,\allowbreak 0.003,\allowbreak 0.005,\allowbreak 0.007,\allowbreak 0.009\}$,
$\lambda_{\mathrm{set}}$ in
$\{0,\allowbreak 0.005,\allowbreak 0.01,\allowbreak 0.02,\allowbreak 0.04,\allowbreak 0.05,\allowbreak 0.10,\allowbreak 0.20\}$, and $\lambda_c$ in
$\{0,\allowbreak 0.01,\allowbreak 0.02,\allowbreak 0.04,\allowbreak 0.05,\allowbreak 0.10,\allowbreak 0.20,\allowbreak 0.40\}$.
For the baselines, we tune hyperparameters within the ranges
specified in their original papers.

\subsection{Overall Performance}

Table~\ref{tab:overall} compares ReCoBR with ten baselines.
We draw the following observations:
\begin{itemize}
\item ReCoBR achieves the best results across all datasets and
metrics. Its Recall@20 improves over the strongest baseline by
2.17\%, 2.94\%, and 4.60\% on Youshu, iFashion, and NetEase,
respectively. The largest relative NDCG gains occur on iFashion. One possible explanation is that iFashion has more U-B interactions
per user, which may help ReCoBR select useful connections for graph
propagation.
\item SGL, XSimGCL, and LightGCL outperform LightGCN across all
datasets and metrics. On iFashion, they also exceed BGCN and
MIDGN despite using only U-B interactions. These results suggest that effective U-B preference learning remains
important even when auxiliary item-level information is available.
\item ReCoBR also outperforms CrossCBR, MultiCBR,
DCDL, and EpicCBR, which model bundle preferences and
integrate information across views. ReCoBR further considers
which U-B connections should guide learning across views, bringing interaction refinement
and auxiliary-information integration into one framework.
ReCoBR's consistent gains across all three datasets support
the value of diffusion-driven graph reconstruction in
uncovering informative U-B preference signals for cross-view
learning.
\end{itemize}


\subsection{Ablation Study}

Table~\ref{tab:ablation} compares ReCoBR with five variants:
\textbf{w/o Graph Recon.} restores the original U-B graph;
\textbf{w/o SetLoss} removes the bundle-set consistency loss;
\textbf{w/o Pre-fusion CL} and \textbf{w/o Post-fusion CL}
remove their respective contrastive objectives; and
\textbf{w/o All CL} removes both. Graph reconstruction
improves ranking performance, particularly on iFashion,
supporting preference-guided selection of propagation
connections. SetLoss yields further gains, reinforcing the
value of consistency with historical bundle preferences.
Removing pre-fusion learning also degrades performance,
highlighting the value of connecting U-B preferences with
auxiliary views before fusion. The larger losses without
post-fusion or all contrastive learning emphasize the
importance of representation regularization. Overall, ReCoBR
outperforms all variants, demonstrating the effectiveness of
jointly refining U-B preference signals and integrating
auxiliary information for bundle recommendation.










\subsection{In-depth Analysis}




\paragraph{Interaction Selection Strategies.}
On iFashion, we compare ReCoBR with the original graph
(\textbf{Original UB}) and three filtering strategies: random
selection (\textbf{Random-K}), bundle popularity
(\textbf{Popularity-K}), and pretrained user-bundle similarity
(\textbf{Pretrain-Sim}). All filtering strategies select from
users' training histories under the same per-user retention
limit $\kappa$. As shown in
Figure~\ref{fig:interaction-selection}, ReCoBR consistently
performs best. Random-K can improve over Original UB but
remains inferior to ReCoBR, indicating that selecting
appropriate connections matters beyond reducing their number.
Popularity-K's weak NDCG suggests that global popularity is
insufficient for personalized ranking. Pretrain-Sim performs worse than Random-K, suggesting that
pretrained user-bundle similarity alone may not identify the most
useful connections for graph propagation. Together, these results suggest that preference-guided selection is
more effective than random, popularity-based, or
pretrained-similarity-based selection.













\paragraph{Performance on Sparse Users.}
NetEase users are sorted by increasing training U-B
interaction counts and divided into four equally sized groups
(G1--G4). We compare ReCoBR with \textbf{w/o Pre-fusion CL},
retaining graph reconstruction in both.
Figure~\ref{fig:sparse-users} shows improvements
across all four groups, with the largest relative gains in
the sparsest group G1: 12.64\% in Recall@20 and 9.32\% in
NDCG@20. These results suggest that explicit cross-view
learning is particularly useful when direct bundle feedback
is limited, allowing U-I behavior and B-I composition to
provide additional learning signals for bundle preference
modeling.







\paragraph{Robustness to Noisy U-B Interactions.}
We compare ReCoBR with CrossCBR and MultiCBR using the same
perturbed U-B input graph for each dataset and noise ratio.
Ranking supervision, U-I and B-I relations, and evaluation data
remain unchanged. We rerun ReCoBR's full training pipeline,
including U-B pretraining. Figure~\ref{fig:noise-robustness} shows
that ReCoBR maintains the highest Recall@20 and NDCG@20 across
all datasets and tested noise ratios, showing that it retains
its recommendation advantage when additional random connections
interfere with U-B graph propagation.







\subsection{Hyperparameter Sensitivity}

Figure~\ref{fig:hyperparameter} reports ReCoBR's sensitivity
to five key hyperparameters. We vary one parameter at a time
while keeping the others fixed. For $\beta_T$, intermediate
noise levels yield better performance than the extremes,
suggesting that preference recovery benefits from moderate
perturbation without excessively disrupting historical
information. For $\kappa$, iFashion favors smaller retained
neighborhoods, whereas NetEase benefits from retaining more
connections. This difference highlights the need to balance
neighborhood coverage with preference relevance when selecting
propagation connections. Nonzero $\lambda_{\mathrm{set}}$
weights improve performance on Youshu, while iFashion and
NetEase show only small, nonmonotonic changes. Increasing
this weight does not consistently yield further gains.
Moderate nonzero values of $\lambda_a$ and $\lambda_c$ improve
performance, while larger weights can reduce it. The decline
is especially pronounced for $\lambda_c$ on Youshu and
NetEase, suggesting the need to balance contrastive
regularization with the ranking objective.



\section{Conclusion}

In this paper, we proposed ReCoBR to address noisy and sparse U-B
feedback in bundle recommendation. ReCoBR refined users' historical
bundle preferences through user-conditioned diffusion with a
bundle-set consistency loss. The refined preferences guided the
selection of observed U-B connections to reconstruct the graph for
propagation. Cross-view contrastive learning aligned user and bundle
representations from the reconstructed U-B view with those from the
U-I and B-I views. Experiments on three public datasets showed that
ReCoBR outperformed the compared baselines, and ablation results
supported the effectiveness of its main components.
