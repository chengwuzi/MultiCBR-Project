Diffusion-Driven Graph Reconstruction and Cross-View Contrastive Learning for Bundle Recommendation

Abstract

Bundle recommendation integrates bundle-level feedback and item-level information to model users' preferences for sets of items. Recent methods strengthen user and bundle representations by combining complementary views of user-bundle (U-B) interactions, user-item behavior, and bundle composition through graph modeling, representation fusion, and contrastive learning. Despite this progress, two challenges remain concerning the quality and availability of U-B feedback: (1) noisy U-B interactions can spread misleading signals through graph propagation; (2) sparse U-B feedback limits preference learning for users with few bundle interactions. To address these challenges, we propose ReCoBR, a framework that combines diffusion-driven graph reconstruction with cross-view contrastive learning. We develop a U-B graph denoising pipeline that integrates pretraining, user-conditioned latent diffusion, and graph reconstruction. Starting from pretrained user and bundle embeddings, we refine each user's aggregated historical bundle representation through conditional denoising and use the refined preference to re-score observed interactions and reconstruct the U-B graph. Within this pipeline, a bundle-set consistency loss complements preference reconstruction by encouraging agreement with the user's historical bundle representations. We further introduce pre-fusion cross-view contrastive learning anchored in the reconstructed U-B view. On both the user and bundle sides, the objectives connect U-B representations with their user-item and bundle-item counterparts before fusion, enabling behavioral and compositional information to support preference learning under sparse U-B feedback. Experiments on three public benchmarks show that ReCoBR outperforms the compared baselines.


1 Introduction

Bundle recommendation (BR) aims to recommend collections of related items, such as music playlists and fashion outfits, according to users' preferences [1,2]. In this work, we focus on ranking predefined bundles. A user's interest in a bundle can be informed by both previous choices of complete bundles and preferences for the items they contain. Modeling these two sources of evidence is therefore central to learning personalized bundle preferences.

Early studies incorporate item-level information into bundle preference modeling through joint learning and representation aggregation. DAM, for example, jointly learns from user-bundle (U-B) and user-item (U-I) interactions and uses attention to aggregate constituent items into bundle representations [1]. Graph-based methods further exploit collaborative relations among users, bundles, and items. BGCN separates bundle-level and item-level preference learning: graph propagation captures collaborative signals from U-B and U-I interactions, while bundle-item (B-I) affiliations connect items to their bundles [3]. These approaches enrich the evidence for bundle preference estimation by combining direct bundle feedback with item-level behavior and bundle composition.

Beyond constructing these representations, subsequent work investigates how different views can cooperate during learning. CrossCBR introduces cross-view contrastive learning to encourage mutual enhancement between bundle-view and item-view representations [2]. DGMAE explores another form of cooperation by distilling bundle-level relational knowledge into an item-view graph masked autoencoder [7]. MultiCBR further models U-B, U-I, and B-I relations as three distinct views, allowing bundle composition patterns to be learned through graph propagation [4]. It fuses the view-specific representations for prediction, capturing both within-view and cross-view preferences, and applies contrastive learning in the fused space. Together, these developments strengthen bundle preference learning by coordinating the behavioral and compositional evidence available across views.

Despite these advances, multi-view bundle recommendation still relies on observed U-B interactions to construct the bundle-view graph and supervise bundle ranking. Consequently, the quality of these interactions and the limited feedback available for some users remain important constraints, giving rise to two challenges.

Challenge 1: Noise in user-bundle interactions. As implicit feedback, observed U-B interactions can include choices that do not faithfully reflect users' preferences [5]. When unreliable interactions enter the U-B graph, their influence can spread through neighborhood aggregation and distort the representations used for recommendation [6]. Refining this graph therefore calls for assessing how well observed connections support a user's bundle preferences. Historical bundle choices provide a personalized context for this assessment, yet that context is also derived from imperfect feedback. The difficulty is to use historical preference information to identify useful propagation connections while preserving the bundle-level evidence needed for subsequent learning.

Challenge 2: Sparse user-bundle feedback. For users with few U-B interactions, direct feedback provides only a narrow basis for learning personalized bundle preferences. U-I behavior and B-I composition offer additional evidence, but they describe preferences for individual items and bundle structure, whose relevance to complete-bundle choices must be learned. Interest in a constituent item, for example, does not necessarily imply preference for the whole bundle [7]. The challenge is to connect this complementary evidence to bundle-level representation learning, enabling users with limited histories to benefit from information beyond their observed bundle interactions.

To address these challenges, we propose ReCoBR (Reconstruction and Contrastive Learning for Bundle Recommendation), which combines diffusion-driven U-B graph reconstruction with cross-view contrastive learning. The graph reconstruction module first pretrains user and bundle embeddings on the U-B graph [8] and aggregates each user's historical bundle embeddings into a preference representation. Drawing on diffusion-based recommendation [9,10], we train a latent denoiser to recover this representation from perturbations, conditioned on the pretrained user embedding. Because subsequent interaction scoring compares the recovered preference with individual historical bundle embeddings, we supplement preference reconstruction with a bundle-set consistency loss that encourages directional agreement with these embeddings. We then refine the historical preference representation and use it to re-score observed interactions, retaining the highest-scoring connections to reconstruct the U-B propagation graph. The original U-B interactions continue to provide ranking supervision.

To further support preference learning under limited U-B feedback, we introduce cross-view contrastive learning anchored in the reconstructed U-B view. Within the three-view recommendation backbone [4], we pair U-B representations with their U-I and B-I counterparts before fusion, on both the user and bundle sides. These objectives jointly optimize the paired representations, connecting auxiliary behavioral and compositional information to bundle-level learning. The resulting constraints complement the backbone's representation fusion and post-fusion contrastive learning. The framework thus refines how observed bundle interactions are used for propagation and how auxiliary information supports learning from limited bundle feedback.

Our main contributions are as follows:

• We develop a diffusion-driven U-B graph reconstruction framework that integrates pretraining, user-conditioned historical preference refinement, and observed-interaction selection, supplemented by a bundle-set consistency objective.
• We introduce cross-view contrastive objectives that use the reconstructed U-B view as a common anchor for user-side and bundle-side learning from auxiliary views before fusion.
• Experiments on three public datasets demonstrate ReCoBR's recommendation improvements and examine the contributions of graph reconstruction and cross-view learning through ablations, interaction-selection comparisons, and user-group analysis.
