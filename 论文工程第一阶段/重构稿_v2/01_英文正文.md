# DiGO: Diffusion-Driven Graph Optimization for Bundle Recommendation

## Abstract

Bundle recommendation integrates bundle-level feedback and item-level information to model users' preferences for sets of items. Despite advances in multi-view modeling, two challenges remain: (1) noisy user-bundle (U-B) interactions can introduce misleading signals into graph propagation, distorting the representations used to learn bundle preferences; (2) sparse U-B feedback provides limited evidence for personalized preference learning, making it difficult to characterize users with few historical bundle interactions. To address these challenges, we propose DiGO, a diffusion-driven graph optimization framework for bundle recommendation. First, we develop a U-B graph denoising pipeline that integrates pretraining, user-conditioned latent diffusion, and graph reconstruction. Starting from pretrained user and bundle embeddings, we refine each user's aggregated historical bundle representation through conditional diffusion denoising and use it to re-score observed interactions and reconstruct the U-B graph. Within this pipeline, we tailor the denoising objective to bundle recommendation through a bundle-set consistency loss that encourages the recovered preferences to retain the semantics of the user's historical bundle set. Second, we introduce pre-fusion cross-view contrastive learning anchored in the reconstructed U-B view. User-side and bundle-side objectives connect this view to the user-item and bundle-item views before representation fusion, enabling auxiliary information to strengthen preference learning under sparse U-B feedback. Experiments on three public benchmarks show that DiGO outperforms the compared baselines. Ablation and interaction-selection analyses support the proposed designs, while the user-group analysis shows the largest relative gains from the cross-view objective for users with the fewest bundle interactions.

## 1 Introduction

Users often seek a coherent collection of items rather than a single product. A coordinated outfit or a themed playlist, for example, can satisfy a need that would otherwise require users to assemble suitable items themselves. Bundle recommendation addresses this demand by matching users with sets of related items [1], [2]. Unlike individual-item recommendation, it must capture preferences for a bundle as a whole while accounting for its constituent items. We focus on ranking predefined bundles using historical interactions and bundle composition information.

This setting involves three relations with distinct roles. User-bundle (U-B) interactions record users' choices of complete bundles and provide direct evidence of bundle preferences. User-item (U-I) interactions reflect preferences for individual items, whereas bundle-item (B-I) affiliations describe how items constitute bundles. Representations learned from these relations offer complementary views of users and bundles: the U-B view captures bundle-level collaborative behavior, while the U-I and B-I views contribute item-level behavioral and compositional information [3], [4], [5]. Learning across these views allows a recommender to exploit information beyond users' observed bundle choices.

Early approaches use attention to aggregate constituent items and jointly learn from bundle and item interactions [3]. Graph neural networks (GNNs) extend this modeling to higher-order connectivity among users, bundles, and items [4]. More recently, contrastive learning (CL) has provided a mechanism for connecting different views. CrossCBR encourages agreement between bundle-level and item-level representations through cross-view objectives [1]. MultiCBR separately models all three relations, fuses their representations, and applies contrastive learning after fusion [5]. These approaches demonstrate the value of multi-view information. Nevertheless, learning bundle preferences still faces two challenges arising from noise in observed U-B interactions and limited feedback from individual users.

**Challenge 1: Noise in user-bundle interactions.** Implicit feedback can include observations that do not faithfully reflect user preferences [6]. When such interactions enter the U-B graph, their influence may spread through neighborhood aggregation and affect the representations used for bundle ranking [7]. The difficulty lies in reassessing individual connections in the context of a user's historical bundle set. Each edge records an interaction with one bundle, whereas the representations of multiple interacted bundles jointly provide evidence about the user's bundle preferences. Simply reducing the number of edges does not determine which connections should be retained. The challenge is to exploit this personalized historical context to suppress noisy connections while preserving relevant bundle-preference information.

**Challenge 2: Sparse user-bundle feedback.** Users with few bundle interactions provide limited evidence for learning personalized bundle preferences. U-I behavior and B-I composition offer complementary information, but their relevance to bundle choice must be learned: liking individual items is not equivalent to preferring a complete bundle, and bundle composition alone does not establish a user's interest. CrossCBR aligns bundle-view and item-view representations through cross-view contrastive objectives, whereas DGMAE transfers first- and higher-order U-B knowledge to item-view representation learning through distillation [1], [8]. Their reported effectiveness demonstrates the value of linking bundle-level evidence with item-level behavioral and compositional information. The challenge is therefore to establish explicit associations between bundle-level and auxiliary-view representations, allowing item-level information to support preference learning when direct U-B feedback is limited.

To address these challenges, we propose **DiGO**, a diffusion-driven graph optimization framework for bundle recommendation. **First, we develop a U-B graph denoising framework that integrates pretraining, user-conditioned latent diffusion, and graph reconstruction.** We pretrain user and bundle embeddings on observed U-B interactions, then aggregate each user's historical bundle embeddings into a latent preference vector. Following the diffusion denoising formulation [9], [10], we perturb this vector with progressively increasing noise and learn to recover it using the pretrained user embedding as a personalized condition. Within this framework, we adapt the denoising objective to bundle recommendation by introducing a bundle-set consistency loss (SetLoss). This constraint encourages the recovered preference vector to remain semantically consistent with the user's historical bundle representations. By limiting drift from this bundle-level preference context during recovery, SetLoss helps preserve preference-relevant information for subsequent interaction re-scoring and graph reconstruction. We then use the refined preference vector to re-score observed interactions and select connections for reconstructing the U-B bipartite graph. The complete pipeline thus turns personalized preference denoising into a refinement of the graph used for recommendation.

**Second, we introduce pre-fusion cross-view contrastive learning anchored in the reconstructed U-B view to address sparse bundle feedback.** The recommendation backbone learns user and bundle representations from the reconstructed U-B graph and the U-I and B-I relations, then fuses the view-specific representations for prediction. Before fusion, we construct U-B–U-I and U-B–B-I contrastive pairs on both the user and bundle sides, using the reconstructed U-B view as their common anchor. These objectives encourage agreement between representations of the same entity across paired views while distinguishing different entities. By explicitly connecting auxiliary information to bundle-level representations, they provide additional learning signals beyond each user's limited U-B history. The two modules therefore have distinct but connected roles: the first refines the U-B connections used for propagation, and the second uses representations learned from that graph to support preference learning across views.

Our main contributions are as follows:

- We propose a diffusion-driven U-B graph denoising framework that integrates pretraining, user-conditioned latent diffusion, and interaction selection to reconstruct the graph used for recommendation. Within this framework, we adapt the denoising objective to bundle recommendation through a bundle-set consistency loss that encourages semantic consistency with historical bundle preferences.
- We introduce pre-fusion cross-view contrastive learning anchored in the reconstructed U-B view on both the user and bundle sides. By connecting U-B representations with U-I and B-I representations, this design exploits auxiliary information to alleviate the learning difficulties caused by sparse bundle feedback.
- Experiments on three public benchmarks demonstrate DiGO's effectiveness through baseline comparisons, ablations, and interaction-selection analyses. A user-group analysis further shows that the proposed cross-view objective yields the largest relative gains for the lowest-interaction group.

## 2 Related Work

### 2.1 Bundle Recommendation

Bundle recommendation includes both ranking existing bundles and generating new item combinations [11]. For predefined-bundle ranking, a central issue is how to combine bundle-level interaction evidence with information about constituent items. DAM addresses this issue through attentive item aggregation and joint learning of U-B and U-I interactions [3]. BGCN uses graph propagation to capture bundle-level and item-level collaborative information [4]. In the broader collaborative filtering setting, LightGCN demonstrates the effectiveness of simplified neighborhood aggregation [12]. Subsequent bundle models enrich the preferences and composition patterns captured by graph representations. MIDGN disentangles user intents across global and local views [13], and BundleGT uses a hierarchical graph transformer to model bundling strategies [14].

Beyond representation construction, another line of research focuses on cooperation across views. CrossCBR contrasts bundle-view and item-view representations to encourage mutual enhancement [1]. MultiCBR explicitly models B-I relations alongside U-B and U-I relations, and combines representation fusion with post-fusion contrastive learning [5]. DGMAE instead distills first- and higher-order U-B knowledge into an item-view graph masked autoencoder [8]. These studies establish several ways to exploit auxiliary information for bundle preference learning. DiGO builds on this multi-view setting by refining the U-B graph through conditional latent diffusion and using the reconstructed view to anchor cross-view learning before fusion.

### 2.2 Denoising and Diffusion for Recommendation

Interaction denoising seeks to reduce the influence of observed feedback that does not accurately reflect user preferences. Adaptive Denoising Training identifies potentially noisy feedback through training losses and reduces its influence using loss truncation or reweighting [6]. Diffusion models provide another way to learn from corrupted inputs by reversing controlled perturbations [9]. DiffRec applies this formulation to interaction-vector reconstruction and introduces a latent-space variant for efficiency [10]. DiffuRec operates on target-item embeddings and uses interaction sequences to condition their recovery for next-item prediction [15]. These methods illustrate that the denoising object and conditioning information must be chosen to suit the recommendation task.

Diffusion has also been applied to bundle recommendation. DCBR combines conditional U-B interaction denoising with disentangled contrastive learning [16]. RDiffBR uses residual diffusion on item-level bundle embeddings to accommodate changes in bundle composition [17]. DiGO instead focuses on the connection between historical bundle-preference modeling and U-B graph reconstruction. It uses pretrained user representations to condition the denoising of aggregated historical bundle embeddings, and translates the refined preferences into observed-edge selection. Within this pipeline, the bundle-set consistency loss adapts the denoising objective to historical bundle semantics, supporting the reconstruction of the graph used for downstream learning.

### 2.3 Contrastive Learning for Recommendation

Contrastive learning derives learning signals by encouraging agreement between related representations while distinguishing unrelated ones. SimCLR demonstrates the importance of view construction and contrastive objectives for representation learning [18]. For graph-based recommendation, SGL creates perturbed graph views through operations such as edge and node dropout, then contrasts representations of the same node across views [7]. Knowledge-aware methods extend this principle to structured auxiliary information. KGCL uses consistency under knowledge-graph perturbations to guide interaction augmentation [19], while MCCLK connects collaborative, semantic, and structural representations through multi-level cross-view objectives [20].

The bundle-specific methods discussed above apply contrastive learning to views defined by different user, bundle, and item relations [1], [5]. DiGO follows this approach with a targeted choice of contrastive pairs: U-B–U-I and U-B–B-I for both users and bundles, formed before representation fusion. The reconstructed U-B view provides a common reference for jointly optimizing the paired representations. This links item-level behavioral and compositional information to bundle-level preference learning, helping the model exploit auxiliary evidence under sparse U-B feedback.
